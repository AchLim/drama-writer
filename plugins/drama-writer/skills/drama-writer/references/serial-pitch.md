# Serial pitch: drafting a good serial story as a synopsis pack

Load when the user wants to draft a short-drama series at synopsis level: a pitch, a festival or platform submission, a "first five episodes" pack, or a story that has to make a viewer want the next episode every time. This is an opt-in mode and its own workflow (H). It replaces the depth of Workflow B for this request: no full bible, no act structure, no scripts. Genre, premise, character, dialogue and continuity rules stay in force. Never apply it unasked.

The format comes from short-drama festival and platform briefs: a reviewer reads a few pages, and the writing is judged on whether each episode causes the next. Everything below is a default. If the user's brief names a different episode count, length or language, follow the brief and say what you changed.

## 1. Defaults and parameters

| Parameter | Default | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Episodes | 5 | Use the brief's count when one is named. |
| Length per episode | 120 to 200 words (about 300 to 450 Korean characters) | Situation, event, change and ending must all be legible. No dialogue transcripts, no shot directions. |
| Language | The brief's required language, else the writing-language rules in SKILL.md §1 | A brief that requires English wants English even if the user writes in another language. |
| Extras | None | Add free/paid tags, a paywall block or a platform form only if the user asks for them. |

Output is prose synopses, not a screenplay. Do not add scene IDs, shot IDs, camera specs or performance tags.

## 2. Drafting order

Draft in this order, because each step constrains the next. Deliver the result in the form of `templates/serial-pitch.md`.

1. **Logline.** One or two sentences naming who, the situation, the conflict, and the mechanism that makes it unlike the generic version (`storytelling.md` §8). If you cannot name the mechanism, the premise is still a category.
2. **Leads (2 to 4).** For each, a want, what blocks it, and a relationship to the others. A character who only exists to explain the plot gets cut or merged.
3. **Direction of the whole story.** How it begins, the main conflict, and where it is headed. Decide the direction before the episodes so they escalate toward something. Show it; do not outline every beat.
4. **The chain table** (§5). Fill in each episode's ending event before you write any prose.
5. **The entries** (§3), one per episode.
6. **The checks** (§10).

## 3. The episode entry

Write each entry as **event, then change, then curiosity**:

- **Situation:** where this episode starts, carrying over the emotional state of the last one.
- **Key event:** what happens to the characters, in order, with a cause for each step.
- **Conflict and change:** what shifts because of it (a relationship, a power balance, what someone knows, what they now have to lose).
- **Ending:** the specific event the episode stops on, written out, and the question it leaves.

A title for each episode, written like a plain description of the turn, helps a reviewer skim.

## 4. Concrete events only

A synopsis sentence names who did what to whom. Rewrite every abstract line until it does.

| Abstract | Concrete |
|---|---|
| "The two grow closer." | "She drives him to the hospital and waits in the corridor until his sister arrives." |
| "They fall into danger." | "The loan shark's driver parks outside her shop and waits." |
| "Tension rises between them." | "He cancels the lease she co-signed, and tells her by text." |
| "She uncovers the truth." | "She finds the transfer receipts in his late father's desk, all to the same account." |

A good test: could a reader draw a still of this sentence? If not, it is still a theme.

## 5. The chain rule

Each episode's ending question must be taken up by the next episode's opening, then answered, complicated, or paid with a cost. Fill this table before writing prose:

| Ep | Ending (the event) | The next episode opens by |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | | |
| 2 | | |

- Link the episodes with "therefore" and "but", not "and then". If two episodes could swap places, they are not chained.
- The answer to an ending can itself open a bigger question. Open the next question before closing the current one.
- One event can span two episodes (a countdown in one, its result in the next) when new pressure joins it each time. See `examples/serial-pitch-synopsis.md`.
- Never answer a cliffhanger by resetting it ("it was only a dream", "he got away offscreen").

## 6. The opening hook (Episode 1)

The opening scene decides whether the viewer stays, so the synopsis must say what the viewer sees and hears first. Open on a crisis the protagonist is already in, a shocking event, or an unexpected line. Write it as one concrete sentence with the stake inside it. Do not spend the first sentence on backstory. Follow `hooks-and-cliffhangers.md` for the first-watch clarity test.

## 7. Episode endings

Every episode ends on a stated event that makes the viewer want the next one. Five working types:

- **Crisis:** the protagonist is trapped with no way out.
- **Twist:** a trusted character's true identity or motive is revealed.
- **Choice:** the protagonist makes an irreversible decision.
- **Secret:** the episode stops just before an important truth comes out.
- **Relationship:** two characters' relationship shifts in a direction nobody expected.

Not every episode needs a huge twist. A new crisis that grows out of the episode's own events works. Rotate the types across the pack so the pattern is not visible, and write the ending as the event itself, never as "to be continued". Name the move: who does what, to whom, with what. See `hooks-and-cliffhangers.md` §6 and §7 and `short-form-drama.md` for variety.

## 8. Escalation across the pack

- Each episode changes at least one thing for good: what someone knows, who holds power, what a lead can lose.
- Raise the price before you pay a want. The nearer the exposure or the win, the more the lead risks by going for it (`plot-structure.md` §7).
- Pay something every episode, even if small, and open a bigger question as you pay it.
- A pack is a window onto a longer story. The last episode answers its own question and still leaves the larger one open.
- If an episode repeats the previous conflict at the same size, merge it or cut it.

## 9. Failure patterns

- Abstract summaries with no event.
- The same conflict repeated each episode at the same size.
- "To be continued" instead of a written ending.
- A vague threat as the ending ("his father begins a new scheme").
- A recap episode that adds no information or change.
- Early episodes that resolve everything, leaving nothing to carry forward, or episodes that stall and then jump to a different story.
- A cliffhanger that depends on a character acting stupidly, or on information held back only for convenience.
- Characters who are only plot machinery.

## 10. Checks before returning

Run these silently, and fix what fails:

- Does Episode 1 open on a clear event that a cold reader would find gripping?
- Is it clear what the protagonist wants and what blocks them?
- Does a concrete event happen in every episode, and does the story move?
- Does every episode end on a written event, and do the types vary?
- Does each ending feed the next opening (the chain table holds)?
- Does each episode change something that stays changed?
- Is every entry inside the length budget, and in the required language?
